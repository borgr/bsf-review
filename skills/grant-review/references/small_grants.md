# What changes when the grant is small

Most advice about grant writing is written for the large, prestigious schemes: ERC, NIH R01s,
NSF CAREER. Most grants applied for are smaller: national science foundations,
binational foundations, start-up and seed grants, collaborative pilot funds, and
society and foundation awards. The rules in `proposal_craft.md` still hold. What changes is
the reader, the way scores combine, and what a credible ambition looks like for the money.
Advice tuned to a large scheme gets four things wrong at a small one, and this file is about
those four.

This file is the narrative version of one corner of `grant_types.md`. That file breaks the same
differences into nine dimensions, places other schemes on them, and gives the evidence and
tests per dimension. Read it when a call is small on some dimensions and large on others.

The reference case throughout is the BSF Research Grants programme
(`bsf-review/references/BSF_writing_guide.md`), because its rules are documented down to the
reviewer's grade scale. Treat each point as a question to check against your own call, not as
a fact about it.

## 1. The reader is an expert, and reads everything

Large schemes often have a generalist stage that sees only part of the proposal. Small schemes
usually do not. The typical shape is a handful of **external expert referees** (the BSF uses 3
to 5, usually about four), each reading the whole package, followed by a **panel** that ranks
proposals largely from those reviews.

What follows:

- **No page can be written for the non-specialist alone.** The first page must still state a
  question a panel member from a neighbouring field can carry into the meeting. That reader
  knows the proposal mostly through the reviews and the abstract. But every technical page is
  read by someone who knows the method.
- **Technical credibility is scored from the first page.** A hand-waved step that a
  generalist would skim is the step an expert referee quotes in the review.
- **You often choose some of the referees.** Where the funder asks for suggested reviewers,
  the list is a strategic document. Name people who know the method *and* the system, and
  respect the conflict-of-interest rules exactly. One ineligible name can cost you the slot,
  or prompt the funder to discard the list.
- **The panel member who presents your proposal has read it once.** They will summarise it
  from the abstract, the first page and the reviews. Make the first page the summary you
  want repeated.

## 2. Grades combine harshly, so the objection costs more than the brilliance gains

Look up how the funder combines grades. Small, oversubscribed schemes tend to fund only the
proposals that nearly every referee rates at the top. The BSF tells referees that typically
"only applications that score high on the cooperation and receive 'Excellent' merit scores by
a majority of the reviewers" are recommended, and that Excellent is "reserved for near-perfect
proposals where there are few or no critical remarks". Its next grade down, Very Good, is
described as "will likely not be elected for funding".

The consequence is a different optimisation from a large scheme's. **A proposal is lost by its
most plausible objection, not won by its best paragraph.** One reviewer who writes "the
sampling plan does not account for X" moves from Excellent to Very Good, and the majority is
gone.

- **Pre-empt objections where they would be raised.** Put a one-clause fallback inside each
  risky task (craft §9.3), and a sentence answering the obvious alternative explanation next
  to the claim it threatens.
- **Prefer the defended claim to the striking one.** A claim an expert will doubt on first
  reading needs its support *before* any emphasis (`grant-writing`'s ranking gate). At a
  small funder, an unsupported bold claim is the sentence that gets quoted in the review.
- **Presentation is graded.** The BSF's own description of a Good proposal mentions
  "organization and presentation, often also with use of the English language". Unclear
  structure and non-native phrasing cost a grade there, as an explicit part of the scale. A
  language-editing pass is a scoring action, not a courtesy.
- **Read the full scale, not just the top grade.** The bottom grades often say "should not be
  considered for resubmission", which matters for the resubmission budget (§6).

## 3. Ambition is calibrated to the budget

A large programme grant buys a team for five years, and its readers penalise the safe next
step. A small grant buys a student, part of a postdoc, some travel and consumables. Its
readers penalise a promise the money cannot keep.

- **Size the aim to the grant, and say what else pays.** The BSF says of its own grants that
  they are small and "in many cases cannot support the entire research project", so "the
  grant is mostly given to support the cooperation". Where that is true, the proposal can
  describe a larger programme, provided it says plainly which part this grant funds and what
  existing funding, equipment and people cover the rest. The resources section (craft §12)
  becomes part of the scientific case.
- **Step-change rhetoric reads as unrealistic.** Keep the vision to two sentences and spend the
  space on why the aim is achievable. "Transformative" is fine where the funder asks for it,
  as the BSF reviewer form does. Show it as a field-level consequence of a bounded result
  (craft §8), not as a claim about the project's size.
- **Moonshots: one, bounded, or none** (craft §9.4).
- **Feasibility is scored, so demonstrate it.** Unlike an ERC Step-1 synopsis, a small-grant
  research plan is read by referees asked about the "work plan and methodology, including a
  fallback plan". Preliminary data, pilot results, access to the system and to equipment, and
  a realistic timeline are all scored here. Start-up tracks are often explicit that
  preliminary results are not required, which tells you that the regular track expects them.

## 4. Collaboration is a criterion, not a context

Many small grants exist to create or sustain a collaboration: binational foundations,
cross-institutional seed funds, and joint programmes between two agencies. In those
schemes, **the collaboration is scored the way the science is**, and a proposal whose science
is Excellent can still fail on it.

What referees are asked, in the BSF's wording: does the research "in the different
laboratories consist of interdependent, complimentary parts of a single program? Will the
outcomes of the collaboration be more than the sum of the individual efforts?" What that asks
the proposal to show:

- **Interdependence, not adjacency.** Two subprojects that share a topic are not a
  collaboration. Show the dependency: site A's result is site B's input, B's model chooses
  A's next experiment, A's facility makes B's measurement possible. A diagram with arrows
  between sites (craft §9.1) proves it faster than a paragraph.
- **What neither side can do alone.** One sentence per partner, stating a capability, system,
  dataset or instrument, and then the sentence that the question needs both (craft §2.2,
  barriers).
- **Named roles at the task level.** Every task names which group does it (craft §10). A plan
  where "we" does everything reads as one lab's plan with a partner attached.
- **The mechanics.** Exchange visits, co-supervised students, shared data, joint meetings,
  and who integrates the results. Keep it specific: dates, durations, people.
- **Both partners visibly wrote it.** Funders say they can tell. The BSF states that when it
  is "apparent" one side did not help prepare the plan, that counts against the proposal. One
  register throughout, and the second partner's expertise present in the technical sections,
  are the evidence.
- **Past collaboration produced joint output.** Where the funder tracks previous joint grants,
  as the BSF does, a previous grant without joint papers is a liability to address. It is not
  something to omit.

## 5. The extra documents carry weight

A large scheme concentrates the case in one or two long documents. A small scheme often adds
short required pieces that are easy to treat as forms and are not: a 250-word abstract, a
250-word impact statement, a lay summary, letters of collaboration, a schedule form. Three
rules:

- **The abstract is the panel's summary of you.** At a funder where a panel ranks from reviews,
  the abstract is often the only text every panel member reads. It must name the question, the
  approach and the contribution, and, at a collaborative funder, the collaboration.
- **Each short statement answers its own question in the funder's own sense of the word.**
  "Broader impact" at the BSF asks for social, economic or scientific value in about 250
  words. At NSF it is a separately scored criterion with its own expectations. Do not reuse
  one for the other unread.
- **Letters say something.** A letter of collaboration that only confirms participation
  misses the chance to state the partner's role in the partner's own voice, which is exactly
  the evidence §4 asks for.

## 6. Resubmission is budgeted

Small funders return reviews, which makes resubmission the normal route to funding. They also
cap it. The BSF allows one resubmission, a third submission only after one specific
outcome letter, and bars applicants who submit a third time otherwise. Before writing,
know how many submissions this proposal has left, and whether the last outcome letter
permits another. A weak first submission "for the feedback" spends one of a small number of
attempts.

When resubmitting (craft §13), expect a mix of old and new referees. The BSF's resubmission
letter goes to both, and the BSF warns that "reviewers do not want to be overloaded".
Quote only the sentence you answer.

## 7. A short checklist for a small grant

Before the ladder in `review_protocol.md`, confirm these, because each is a place where
large-scheme habits cost a small-scheme proposal:

1. The aim is sized to this budget, and the plan says what else pays for the rest.
2. Every risky task carries its fallback, and preliminary data are present where the track
   expects them.
3. The first page states the question in one sentence a neighbouring-field panel member can
   repeat.
4. If the scheme is collaborative, every task names a site, and one sentence per partner says
   what only they bring.
5. Each short required statement answers its own question in the funder's sense.
6. The suggested-reviewer list obeys every conflict rule.
7. The English has had a dedicated editing pass.
8. You know how many submissions this proposal has left.
