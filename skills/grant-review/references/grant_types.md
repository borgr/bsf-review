# Grant types: the dimensions that change the advice

Two proposals for the same science can need opposite advice. That is not because one funder is
stricter. They differ on a handful of dimensions, and each dimension decides which rules in
`proposal_craft.md` apply with full force. This file names those dimensions, places common
schemes on them, and gives the advice per dimension. It generalises `small_grants.md`, which
was written from one corner of this space.

Use it in two ways. **Before reviewing for a funder without a guide**, place the call on each
dimension from its own documents and read the advice for each position. **When moving a
proposal between funders**, compare the two positions on each dimension. Every dimension
where they differ is a list of edits.

## The dimensions

| # | Dimension | Low end | High end |
|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | **Money per lab-year** | a student and travel (small) | a team (large) |
| D2 | **Risk appetite** | feasibility and fallbacks scored | step change scored, feasibility deferred or discounted |
| D3 | **Who reads** | expert referees read everything | generalists first, experts later or never |
| D4 | **How grades combine** | one objection sinks it (majority-top, unanimity) | averaged, or a panel discussion that can override |
| D5 | **Right of reply** | none: written reviews, never rebutted | interview, rebuttal, or a response stage |
| D6 | **Collaboration** | single PI | multi-PI, with collaboration scored |
| D7 | **Career stage** | open | tracked (start-up, early-career, consolidator) |
| D8 | **Documents** | one plan, everyone reads it | several documents read by different readers |
| D9 | **Attempts** | unlimited, with feedback | capped, or penalised after a weak outcome |

## Per-dimension advice

Each entry says what the dimension changes, the evidence it rests on, and the test the review
skill applies. **Transfers:** says how the advice carries to positions other than the BSF's.
The evidence came from a research round on 29 October 2026. It covered the BSF's own documents
and data, funded NIH applications paired with their summary statements (via NIAID's public
samples), one PI's public series of NSF reviews and panel summaries, funded multi-site NSF
proposals made public, and the official criteria of analogous schemes. `bsf-review`'s guide
§14 lists the sources.

### D1 — Money per lab-year

**What it changes.** The scope a reviewer will believe. At small grants, "too ambitious for the
time and resources" is the critique raised even against funded proposals: an R21 judged nearly
flawless was still called "likely overly ambitious". At large grants, too little ambition is
the critique: a declined five-year CAREER was "not ambitious enough". DFG's form states the
rule directly: the tasks "should correspond to the funds requested".

**Tests.**
- **Count:** at most three objectives on a small grant. More than about six open problems was
  the most common reason given for declined small proposals, cited as "too many problems listed
  in rapid succession".
- Each objective has a deliverable and a success criterion.
- Stretch work is marked as stretch.
- The plan names what else pays: other grants, existing data, equipment and students
  (craft §12).
- **Plan for the typical award, not the cap.** At the BSF the cap is $300,000, but theory-only
  grants typically receive $150,000–200,000.

**Transfers:** fully to any small grant. Inverts at the large end, where the critique becomes
too little ambition.

### D2 — Risk appetite

**What it changes.** Where the proposal spends its argument: on why it could change the field,
or on why it will work.

**Evidence.** In funded modest grants, the novelty is usually **a new combination or
application of a validated method**. Reviewers still list "approach used in other systems" as a
weakness, but treat it as minor when preliminary data show the method works. Two patterns
turned critiques into enthusiasm:

- **A design that pays off either way:** "even the simple comparison … is exciting enough".
- **Thorough alternatives:** one review accepted missing feasibility data as minor because
  "standard alternative approaches are proposed".

NIH's 2025 framework now states the trade-off in its own words. Where feasibility is less
certain, reviewers "evaluate whether the uncertainty is balanced by the potential for major
advances".

**Tests, at the moderate position.**
- A central hypothesis on page 1.
- A working hypothesis per objective.
- An "even if X fails, we obtain Y" sentence for each risky objective.
- Novelty stated against **named** prior work.
- Pitfalls written as if-then items, about 10–20 % of each objective's text.

**At the high position** (ERC, HFSP), the vision carries more weight, feasibility is argued
less in the first document, and one bounded moonshot is welcome (craft §9.4).

### D3 — Who reads

**What it changes.** Whether the first page is written for someone outside the subfield or for
an expert, and how the panel learns what the proposal is.

**Evidence.**
- At the BSF, one adviser summarises 3–5 expert reviews for the panel.
- NSF panel summaries are short syntheses, each repeating a reviewer's sharpest doubt: "hinges on
  finding a statistic with no discussion on techniques to find it". The doubt that reaches the
  panel is a single sentence from one reviewer.

**Tests.**
- **Expert test:** every claim has a sentence on *how*, not only *what*.
- **Summariser test:** the abstract and the first page give the question, the approach, the
  team logic and the contribution.
- **Origin test:** four in five BSF referees are not Israeli, so local systems, sites and
  datasets are introduced.

**Transfers:** the summariser test applies wherever a panel ranks from reviews (BSF, ISF, NSF,
SNSF, FWF). The generalist-first document applies where a stage sees only part of the proposal
(ERC Step 1, Marsden preliminary round).

### D4 — How grades combine

**What it changes.** How much one objection costs.

**Evidence.** NSF and the BSF use the same five words: Excellent, Very Good, Good, Fair, Poor.
They aggregate differently.

- **NSF:** in one PI's public review series, E/VG/G and G/VG were both recommended for funding,
  while VG/VG was declined.
- **BSF regular track:** Excellent is the fundable grade, and Very Good "will likely not be
  elected".

The same reviews that fund at NSF would not fund at the BSF.

The NIH summary statements show the two kinds of critique:

- **Funded** proposals' critiques are acknowledged limitations with a mitigation already in the
  text.
- **Declined** proposals' critiques are missing pieces: no strategy for the hard step, undefined
  success, vague problems.

**Tests.**
- **Objection audit:** list the three most plausible objections, then find each answer in the
  text.
- **Quotable strengths:** give the enthusiastic reviewer two or three sentences to argue from.

**Transfers:** both tests help everywhere. They are decisive only at majority-top schemes.

### D5 — Right of reply

**What it changes.** Where ambiguities are resolved.

- **No reply** (BSF, ISF, NSF): every ambiguity is read against you, once. Pre-emption has to sit
  inside the document, which is why D4's audit matters.
- **A reply exists:** Marsden lets applicants respond to referees before panel grading. The ERC
  interviews at Step 2. NIH's resubmission introduction is a delayed reply. Each moves some
  defence out of the main text.

**Test.** For each objection the audit finds, the answer is in the proposal, not saved for later.

**Transfers:** with a reply stage, keep a list of expected objections and the evidence for each,
ready for the response.

### D6 — Collaboration

**What it changes.** Whether the team is context or a criterion.

**Evidence.** Where collaboration is scored, funders use near-identical language:

- **BSF:** "interdependent, complimentary parts of a single program", "more than the sum of the
  individual efforts".
- **NSF CISE:** "greater than the sum of each of their individual contributions".
- **HFSP:** collaboration must be "absolutely necessary", and "concerted action" is not
  supported.
- **DFG–Israel DIP:** "strength of the scientific cooperation including the exchange of
  scientists".

Funded multi-site proposals made public share these signals:

1. A lead group named for every task.
2. Co-led interface tasks with dates.
3. A roles table.
4. Meeting cadence given in numbers.
5. Trainees attached to one lab with a cross-lab duty.
6. A timeline keyed to objectives.

**Tests.** The seven tests in `bsf-review` guide §7: the removal test, the lead line, the
interface task, the reason for the pairing, mechanics in numbers, two real partners, and money
and letters that agree. The checker flags tasks that name no group.

**Transfers:**
- **Prior collaboration** splits funders. The BSF rewards earlier joint output, while HFSP
  penalises prior co-publication.
- **Governance** text scales with size. NIH leadership plans with arbitration committees look
  disproportionate on a two-lab grant.
- **At single-PI schemes** a collaboration framing can hurt. The ERC funds *your* programme.

### D7 — Career stage

**What it changes.** What substitutes for a track record and preliminary data.

**Evidence.**
- **NIH:** reviewers of early-stage investigators "may expect" fewer data and judge potential.
  For R21s, preliminary data are "not expected".
- **NSF CRII:** adds two review questions. Does the work produce results that make future
  proposals competitive, and is it a "necessary and critical step" to launch the career?
- **Mentored awards:** reviewers penalise a senior partner who drives the science.
- **Contrasts:** Emmy Noether scores independence explicitly. MSCA scores the two-way transfer
  between fellow and host.

**Tests.**
- A feasibility argument even without the PI's own data.
- An exit statement: what the result enables next.
- The senior partner present in the work plan with named tasks.
- The ideas visibly the junior's.
- One sentence on what the junior brings.
- Scope sized to the track's money.

**Transfers:** to every early-career track. The independence test is strongest at schemes that
fund group leadership.

### D8 — Documents

**What it changes.** Where each argument must live.

- **Several documents read by different readers** (ERC B1 and B2): each must stand alone for its
  reader (`review_protocol.md` stage 4).
- **One plan read by everyone** (BSF, SNSF, DFG, NSF): order and proportion do the work.

Structures that converge across single-plan schemes (SNSF, DFG, NSF, and NIH's Approach):

1. State of the art and own preliminary work.
2. Objectives, with a hypothesis.
3. Per-objective methods with expected outcomes, and pitfalls with alternatives.
4. Schedule and milestones.
5. Roles, where there is a team.

Measured shares in funded modest NIH applications:

- **Preliminary work:** 39–40 % of the text in an R03 and an R15.
- **Pitfalls with alternatives:** 0 % in the R03, whose reviewers raised exactly the unanswered
  objections, against 21 % in the R15, whose review praised its prioritisation.

**Tests.**
- A one-paragraph opening box with the question, hypothesis and objectives.
- One block per objective (`bsf-review` guide §4.6).
- An explicit schedule.
- Short required statements, such as abstracts and impact statements, each answering its own
  question.

**Transfers:** the per-objective block transfers to any single plan. The shares are a
comparison, not a target.

### D9 — Attempts

**What it changes.** The cost of a weak submission, and how the resubmission is written.

- **Capped:** BSF allows one resubmission, with a third only after an N3E letter. NIH allows one
  A1, after which the idea returns as new.
- **Penalised:** the ERC bars applicants for a year or more after a weak Step-1 outcome.
- **Free:** NSF has no limit.

The letter's audience also differs. BSF resubmissions go to old and new reviewers. NIH's one-page
introduction goes to whoever is assigned. Since 2024, NIH forbids marking changes in the
resubmitted text.

**Tests.**
- **Gate before drafting:** resubmit if the weaknesses are fixable, reframe if the question, fit
  or collaboration was doubted.
- **The letter:**
  - Opens with a summary of changes.
  - Answers the panel summary first.
  - For each point gives the critique, a direct answer, and where the change is.
  - Contains no defensive phrasing.
  - Claims only changes that are actually made.

The checker covers the summary, the panel, the locations and the defensive phrases for the BSF.

**Transfers:** the letter pattern is general. Page budgets and the rules on marking changes are
funder-specific.

## Placing a new call

For each dimension, find the sentence in the call or the reviewer guidelines that decides it.
Then write the position into the funder guide's reader table (`proposal_craft.md` §1) and its
review overlay (`funder_profile.md`). Where no sentence decides a dimension, say so, and ask the
funder's office or a recent panel member. Do not infer it from a neighbouring scheme.
