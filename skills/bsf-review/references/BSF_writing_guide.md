# Writing a BSF Research Grant

A working reference for the **U.S.–Israel Binational Science Foundation (BSF) Research Grants
programme, 2026 cycle**, covering regular and start-up applications. It is drawn from the BSF's
own *Regulations for Online Submission* (updated August 2026), its *Guidelines for Reviewers*,
its evaluation, statistics and FAQ pages, and the NSF-BSF call documents (§14). It is organised
so that you can read it once for the argument and then work [§15](#15-review-overlay)
against a live draft.

This file holds only what is **specific to the BSF**. The writing rules that hold at any funder
are in `grant-review/references/proposal_craft.md` and are cited as `craft §n`. What changes at
small and collaborative funders in general is in `grant-review/references/small_grants.md`.
Section numbers here are stable, so `BSF §7` in a checker finding or a margin note always means
§7 of this file.

## 1. What the BSF funds

The BSF "supports bilateral, collaborative research on basic science topics by U.S. and Israeli
scientists that are conducted in both countries". Three things in that sentence do work:

- **Basic science.** A knowledge question, not a product (craft §4). Applied work is fundable
  where it answers a question.
- **Bilateral and collaborative.** Every application has at least one PI from an Israeli
  institution and one from a U.S. institution, and at most six in total. They present "a
  single, coordinated application, in which the roles and tasks of the respective partners are
  clearly defined". Cooperation is a scored criterion (§6, §7), not an eligibility box.
- **Conducted in both countries.** Research done entirely in one lab, with the other partner
  advising, reads as the wrong scheme.

**What it will not fund or does not invite:** preparing or publishing books or monographs;
infrastructure or standard equipment; work in areas not open this year (§2), which is rejected;
and applications involving, directly or indirectly, the Arab–Israeli conflict, which "should be
discussed with the BSF staff before submission". A proposal a panel judges below "expected
standards of scientific or intellectual merit" **can be desk-rejected without external review**.

Prior collaboration between the PIs is not required, and "the establishment of new research
teams is welcomed". The FAQ says a previous collaboration is not weighed either way, **unless**
it was a previous BSF grant, in which case the BSF checks whether it produced joint output
(§10).

Numbers in brackets are proposals evaluated. Expect about one in four in a normal year. Small
fields swing widely between cycles.

**Your real deadline is the grants office's.** The final submission is an online approval by the
initiator's research authority, and the BSF tells PIs to submit "early enough to allow your grant
office to go over the application". Every PI approves first, then the initiator approves last,
then the research authority. The initiator's authority can unlock the application for revisions
until it gives that final approval.

**Fields alternate by year.** Even years (2026, 2028): chemistry, computer sciences, earth, ocean
and atmospheric sciences, economics, energy, environmental research, materials, mathematics,
physics, sociology and anthropology, and psychology (excluding psychobiology). Odd years (2027,
2029): biomedical engineering, health sciences, life sciences and psychobiology. Sub-areas are
listed in Appendix I of the regulations and panels in Appendix II. **Choose the area by
the panel**, because panels cover several areas. A proposal on a disciplinary boundary may be
moved to another panel, and "decisions to move a proposal between panels are final". If that
risk applies, send the BSF a one-page summary *before* writing.

**Eligibility.**

- PIs hold a PhD, MD or equivalent and are faculty, or equivalent, at a **non-profit** submitting
  institution: a university, government research institute or hospital. Adjunct or affiliate
  faculty qualify if the institution gives them resources and the right to submit. Visiting
  professors, postdocs and students do not.
- A PI at a for-profit organisation may take part, but their research is not funded. U.S.
  government scientists must consult the BSF first, because their institutions often refuse
  the standard contract.
- **No third-country PIs.** A scientist from a third country may be an unpaid consultant only,
  with no funds, "not even for travel" (FAQ).
- **The BSF does not use "co-PI".** Every investigator is a PI, whether or not they request
  funds, unless the PIs define them as an unpaid advisor.

**One at a time.**

- One application per investigator per annual competition, and one active BSF Research Grant
  at a time. A current grantee may apply only during the **last year** of the active grant.
- A BSF Research Grant and an NSF-BSF grant may be held together if the projects do not
  significantly overlap. You may submit the same project to both, but if both are approved
  only the NSF-BSF one is funded (§13).
- A researcher who is a PI on another regular BSF application or grant can join only as a
  **consultant**: at most 10 % of their time, no grant money for their own research, a role
  described in item 3 of the research plan, and an optional letter (§10).

**Resubmission limits** are in §11. Read them before the first submission, because they decide
how many attempts the proposal has.

"Appendices or supplementary documents, if submitted, will not be sent to referees." Anything
the case depends on must be in the 15 pages.

**Format rules, which apply to every uploaded file.** DOC or PDF, under 4 MB each. **Font 12 pt**
(smaller allowed only for formulas, captions and symbols, and it must stay readable); **line
spacing 1.5 or 2, single not permitted**; **no more than 38 lines per page**; **margins at
least 2 cm (0.8 in)**; A4, or US Letter if the initiator is in the U.S. The plan is explicit:
"Applications with longer texts, or with fonts smaller than 12-point, line spacing of less than
1.5 lines, or margins of less than 2cm … will not be processed." Not following the format rules
"is likely to lead to rejection of the application".

**The LaTeX trap.** `\onehalfspacing` from `setspace` is "one-and-a-half" in the typographer's
sense: a stretch of about 1.24 at 12 pt, not 1.5. At 12 pt on A4 with 2 cm margins that sets
**about 40 lines per page, over the 38-line cap**. Use `\linespread{1.5}`, which gives about 34
lines, or check the line count on the compiled PDF. `grant_check.py` estimates this from the
source. Word's "1.5 lines" setting does not have this problem.

**What 15 pages buys.** At 12 pt and 1.5 spacing, a page holds roughly 350–420 words of running
text, about half a single-spaced page. Fifteen pages is therefore roughly 5,000–6,000 words
*including figures and preliminary results*. That is a mid-sized proposal, not a long one.
Plan figures early.

**Generative AI.** "Proposers are encouraged to indicate in the project description the extent
to which, if any, generative AI technology was used and how it was used to develop their
proposal." This is encouraged, not required, and the proposers remain responsible for accuracy.
One sentence at the end of the plan does it.

**The final PDF.** The system compiles one PDF, which goes to referees. The initiator must open
it, check it against Appendix III and upload the signed checklist. Read that PDF and not your
source files: a figure that rendered at 9 pt or a table that lost its fonts is what the reviewer
sees.

## 4. The research plan, section by section

The regulations require five items. "Applications lacking any of the information requested will
not be considered."

1. A brief description of the subject and the scientific and technological background.
2. Objectives and significance of the research.
3. Comprehensive description of the methodology and plan of operation, **including the respective
   roles of the Israeli and American principal investigators**.
4. Risk analysis and alternative paths if the plan fails ("only in those fields in which it is
   relevant").
5. An account of available U.S. and Israeli resources, including all personnel and equipment
   relevant to the research.

The file needs "Research Plan" in its heading. "A title is not needed."

**Use the five items as your top-level headings**, in this order, with your own words after
them: "3. Methodology and plan of operation: from single-cell traces to a causal model". A
referee with the reviewer form open (§6) maps their questions to these items, and the panel
checks the items exist. Two additions earn their own headings because they are scored: a
**collaboration section** (§4.7), inside item 3, and **preliminary results** (§4.3), at the end
of item 1.

**The mandated order has one trap.** Item 1 comes first, so a plan written straight down the list
reaches its objectives on page 4 or 5, after the background and the preliminary results. A
referee forms a view of the proposal long before that, and a panel member reading for the summary
meets four pages of background first. The **opening** (§4.1) fixes this: half a page, before item
1, that states the question, the hypothesis, the objectives and the collaboration. Everything
after it then reads as support for a claim the reader already holds.

**One way to divide fifteen pages.** The BSF gives no allocation. Its own 2025 tips ask only
for a well-formulated research question, graphs, and a balance between introduction, plan and
bibliography. The split below is editorial. It is calibrated against the section shares of
funded modest-size proposals whose texts are public: NIH R03, R15 and R21 applications paired
with their summary statements, and NSF projects (§16). In those, preliminary results took about
a quarter to two-fifths of the text, and pitfalls with alternatives took 10–20 % of each aim. The
invariant is that item 3 is the largest and at least as long as items 1 and 2 together (craft §5;
machine-checked as `methods-share`).

| Section | Pages | Share | Rules |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening: question, hypothesis, objectives, why two groups | ½ | 3 % | §4.1 |
| 1. Background and state of the art | 2–2½ | 15 % | §4.2 |
| 1b. Preliminary results that motivate the whole project, from **both** groups | ¾–1 | 6 % | §4.3 |
| 2. Objectives and significance | 1 | 7 % | §4.4 |
| 3. Methodology: overview, design figure, timeline | ½–¾ | 5 % | §4.5 |
| 3. Methodology: one block per objective, each with its own preliminary evidence | 6½–7½ | 47 % | §4.6 |
| 3b. The collaboration: roles, interface tasks, mechanics | ¾–1 | 7 % | §4.7 |
| 4. Risk analysis and alternative paths: cross-cutting risks only | ½–¾ | 4 % | §4.8 |
| 5. Resources: personnel, equipment, facilities, other funding, both sides | ½–¾ | 4 % | §4.9 |
| Closing paragraph, plus the one-line generative-AI note | ¼ | 2 % | §4.10 |

The shares sum to 100 % of fifteen pages, and the page ranges round them. Item 3 as a whole is
about 8½ pages. Preliminary evidence in total, section 1b plus the evidence inside the blocks,
comes to about 2–3 pages, 15–20 %. That is less than the quarter to two-fifths the NIH analogues
spend, because a BSF plan also pays for two groups' background and a collaboration section. If
feasibility is your weakest point, move pages to it from item 1. For a start-up, it shrinks and the feasibility
argument (§12) takes its place. What each element looks like in mathematics, empirical computer
science and the social sciences is in craft §14.

An annotated skeleton with every element below as a slot to fill, in LaTeX, Markdown and Word,
is in `templates/bsf/` at the root of this kit. The checker reports each unfilled slot.

Most subsections below follow one pattern: **what the section must do**, **what a referee looks
for**, **how it fails**, and an example where one helps. The examples are invented.

### 4.1 The opening (½ page)

**What it does.** It is the summary you want the adviser to give the panel (§5). It sits
directly under the "Research Plan" heading, before item 1, as a short unnumbered section or a
framed box. It is not one of the five items, so keep it to about 200 words.

**What a referee looks for:** a question they can repeat, a hypothesis they can test, and the
reason this needs two groups. Its absence is in the definition of "Fair" (§6).

Eight moves, one or two sentences each, in this order. This is the NIH Specific Aims page
compressed to half a page, broad at the start, narrow in the middle and broad again at the
end:

1. **Stakes.** What larger problem the answer matters to. One sentence, not a history.
2. **The question and why it is open.** What specifically is not known, and the barrier that
   has kept it so (craft §2.2–2.3).
3. **Long-term goal.** Where the two groups are heading beyond this grant. One sentence. This is
   the vision, and at the BSF it stays this short (craft §3).
4. **This grant's aim and central hypothesis**, or central question or conjecture where the
   field does not test hypotheses (craft §14).
5. **Why it is testable now.** The enabling result, preferably your own, and whose it is.
6. **The objectives, a line each**, as bold knowledge claims numbered as they will be in item 2.
7. **Why these two groups.** What each side brings that the other lacks, and that the question
   needs both. One or two sentences.
8. **Payoff.** What will be known at the end that is not known now.

The objectives together must test the whole central hypothesis, and nothing beyond it.

**How it fails.** It opens on the field's history. It states the topic but no question. The
aim is a process ("to study …") rather than what will be known. It ends on method detail, with
no payoff. The hypothesis first appears in item 2. The collaboration is absent until item 3.
The objectives are methods ("apply single-cell sequencing to …").

> Weak: "Drought is a major threat to agriculture worldwide. Soil microbial communities play
> important roles in plant health, but their response to drought remains poorly understood."
>
> Better (moves 4, 2 and 7, abridged): "Whether a soil community recovers from drought depends,
> we hypothesise, on its drought history rather than on its composition. No test has been
> possible, because no one has sampled communities with known drought histories under matched
> conditions. The Negev sites of the Be'er Sheva group span 40 years of recorded rainfall, and
> the Davis group's manipulation plots reproduce them experimentally. Together they allow the
> first test."

### 4.2 Item 1: background and state of the art (2–2½ pages)

**What it does.** It builds, for an expert in each half of the project, the argument that the
question is open, hard and now answerable. It ends where the objectives begin.

**What a referee looks for:** that the authors know the field, including its newest work and its
competitors; the closest prior work, named, with what is different here (the reviewer form's
"transformative … not repetitive"); and a gap that is a barrier, not an omission.

- **Two to four sub-areas, each tied to an objective**, each in four beats: what is known →
  its limitation → the barrier → how this project moves past it (craft §5).
- **Both halves of the science.** A collaborative project usually joins two literatures, such as
  a system and a method or a theory and a measurement. Cover both at the same depth. A background
  in one community's terms alone tells the other side's referee that their half is decoration.
- **Introduce what is local.** Four in five referees are outside Israel (§5). An Israeli field
  site, cohort, dataset, regulation or institution gets a clause of explanation, as a method
  would.
- **Say how strong the key prior evidence is.** If the result the project builds on is from one
  study, small, or unreplicated, say so and say how the plan checks it. Experts notice, and an
  unacknowledged weak foundation is a ready objection.
- **Separate this project from the PIs' other work.** Say how it relates to what the groups are
  already doing and funded for. Item 5 then states the non-overlap.
- **The groups' own work belongs inside the argument**, cited where it removes a barrier, not in
  a block of self-citation. Both groups appear.
- **Cite the field fairly and fully.** The BSF uses the bibliography to find referees (§10), so
  the people you cite are candidates to review you. A competitor left out is the referee most
  likely to notice.
- **End on the gap paragraph**: the specific unknown, why it is unknown, and what has changed to
  make it answerable. That paragraph hands over to the preliminary results and item 2.

**How it fails.** A textbook review with no "but", or a mosaic of pasted paragraphs that does
not lead to the experiments. A gap that appears only on the last line. The literature of one
group only. A history of the field that no expert needs. Generic phrases such
as "poorly understood" (craft §2.3, machine-flagged).

### 4.3 Preliminary results (¾–1 page, plus one figure per objective)

**What it does.** It shows that the hypothesis is plausible and that the methods work in these
hands. It also shows that both groups are already doing the science, which answers criterion C
before item 3 begins. Referees are asked about feasibility, so in the regular track this
section is expected.

**Where it goes.** Split it. Put the one or two results that motivate the whole project in a
short subsection at the end of item 1 ("1b. Preliminary results"), where they lead into the
objectives. Put the evidence for each objective inside that objective's block (§4.6), aiming at
one figure or number per objective, so that each claim and its support sit together. NIH review
staff give the same advice for aims. Do not show the same figure twice.

**What a referee looks for:**

- **Results from both groups.** Ideally one joint result, such as a pilot exchange of samples,
  a shared analysis or a co-authored preprint, which is the strongest single piece of evidence
  for criterion C.
- **Each figure with a takeaway caption** that states the finding, not the method. Name the group
  that produced it and give n, error bars and the comparison. Captions may be below 12 pt but
  must be readable.
- **What the data do not settle.** "These data show A, but cannot tell B from C, which is
  Objective 2" (craft §7). That sentence turns evidence into the reason for the grant.
- **Evidence for the new method above all.** Data showing that an untested technique works in
  these hands is worth more than data for a routine one.
- **Proportion.** Enough to make the hard step credible, not so much that the work looks done.

**Field translations** (craft §14). In mathematics and theory: a special case proved, a lemma, a
computation, or the new idea shown to get traction. In computational work: a prototype or pilot
with numbers against a baseline. In the social sciences: a pilot survey, power estimates, and
proof of data access. The regulations ask for preliminary results "if any", but the reviewer
guidelines exempt only start-ups from them, which tells you the regular track expects them. In a
start-up: a feasibility argument from the PI's training, the senior partner's data or a modest
pilot (§12).

**How it fails.** "Unpublished data, not shown". Data from one lab only. A figure with no stated
takeaway. Results that already answer the main question.

### 4.4 Item 2: objectives and significance (1 page)

**What it does.** It states precisely what the project will establish and why that matters. The
opening stated these in one line each. Here they get their hypotheses and their links.

**Objectives.** Two or three, four at most. Item 3 has room for two to two and a half pages
per objective with three of them. A fourth leaves each block too thin for an expert, and an Israeli reviewer
writing for applicants names over-ambition as the common local failing, adding that he knows of
no proposal rejected for being too focused. Each objective gets:

1. **A bold knowledge claim.** "Determine whether …", "Identify the …", "Quantify …". Not "to
   understand" or "to study", and not a deliverable (craft §4). Both are machine-flagged.
2. **Its working hypothesis**: one testable sentence, or a precise question or conjecture.
3. **One sentence of route.** The detail is item 3's job.
4. **Who leads it and what the other group contributes.**
5. **Its link to the others**: what it takes from them, or gives them.

**Interdependent, but not blocking.** NIH advice says aims should be related but not dependent,
so that one failure does not sink the next. The BSF asks for "interdependent, complimentary [sic]
parts of a single program". Both hold when the objectives depend on each other **across the two
groups**, with each group's output feeding the other's question, but not **in sequence**: each
objective yields a result even if another underperforms. Say so in a sentence.

**Do not split objectives by country.** "Objective 1 (Israel): … Objective 2 (U.S.): …" fails the
removal test of §7 on sight. Each objective should need both groups, or the set should interlock
so visibly that removing either group breaks it.

Order the objectives so that risk rises, and say that the project stands without the last one if
it is the risky one (craft §4).

**Significance.** One or two paragraphs, forward-looking, distinct from the background. The
background says what is known. Significance says what changes when the objectives are met: which
belief is confirmed or overturned, which method becomes possible, and which next questions open.
Give the *because* (craft §8). This answers criterion B, so name the consequence for the field
first. Put the social or economic value in one sentence, and leave the rest to the Broader
Impacts Statement (§8).

**How it fails.** Objectives that are the tasks renamed. An objective with only one interesting
outcome: "determine whether A matters" is weaker than "compare the role of A in B and in C",
which informs whichever way it comes out. Five objectives in fifteen pages. Significance that
repeats the background. "This research will have broad impact."

### 4.5 Item 3: the overview (½–¾ page)

**What it does.** It opens item 3 with the logic of the plan, before any task begins (craft §6,
§9.1).

- **One paragraph of logic.** How the objectives follow from each other, and why this structure
  fits the question.
- **One design figure that does two jobs.** Draw objectives as rows and the two groups as
  lanes, with arrows for the samples, data, models and people that cross between them. It shows
  the project is one project (craft §9.1) and that the collaboration is interdependent
  (criterion C, §7) in a single glance.
- **Methods shared by all objectives, stated once.** Common datasets, statistics, validation and
  data sharing go here, so that each objective block does not repeat them.
- **A timeline.** Objectives or tasks against years or half-years, with milestones, decision
  points and **exchange visits** marked. Criterion E asks whether the schedule is "realistic or
  … too ambitious", and the work schedule in the online form must tell the same story (§3).
  Name who does the work in each period, such as "PhD student A (Haifa)" or "postdoc (Austin)",
  because the budget is read against it. **A milestone states a result, not an activity.**
  Restating the aims as milestones is the most common error in NIH milestone plans.

> Weak milestone: "Year 2: complete the experiments of Objective 2."
>
> Better: "Month 18: recovery rates measured for ≥ 30 communities at both sites; the slope decides
> whether Objective 3 tests the history model or the composition model."

**How it fails.** Item 3 starts straight into Task 1.1. The timeline is a uniform bar per
objective lasting all four years. The figure shows boxes with no arrows between the groups.

### 4.6 Item 3: one block per objective (2–2½ pages each)

This is where the grade is decided. Each block is read by the referee who knows that method
best. Use the same structure in every block, so a referee can find each answer:

1. **Rationale and working hypothesis.** Why this objective is needed and what it tests. Two to
   four sentences, restating item 2 only as much as needed.
2. **Preliminary evidence** specific to this objective: a figure, a number or a pointer to section 1b of the plan,
   and which group produced it.
3. **Approach, as tasks.** Each task is a short story (craft §9.3): its purpose, the approach at
   expert level, the check that tells you it worked, and a **lead line**: "Led by the Haifa
   group; the Austin group supplies the simulations from Task 1.1." Say what the task receives
   from the other group. At expert level means the details a referee in this method would ask
   for: controls, sample sizes or power, validation, statistical analysis, the data or
   instrument used, and how data will be shared between the sites. **Spend the detail where the
   risk is.** Reviewers fault both too little detail on new or untested methods and too much on
   routine ones.
4. **Expected outcomes and success criterion.** What result counts as support for the hypothesis,
   and what counts against it, stated before the work is done. "The hypothesis is supported if
   recovery time falls with drought history (slope < 0, 95 % CI) across ≥ 30 communities." In
   theory: which statement is proved, or which special case. In computation: which measurable
   gain over which baseline, on which held-out data, as a research finding rather than an
   engineering output. Say what each plausible outcome would mean, so that the objective informs
   whichever way it goes.
5. **Pitfalls and alternatives**: numbered if-then items, 10–20 % of the block. Each names a
   likely problem, why it is unlikely given the evidence, the concrete alternative, and why the
   objective survives. Cover **both kinds**: technical (the method fails) and interpretive (the
   result has another explanation, and which control or comparison separates them). End with
   what is learned even if the hypothesis fails. "Even if X fails, we obtain Y" is the sentence
   that turned top-rated reviews in the public set from doubt to enthusiasm.

> Weak pitfall: "If problems arise, alternative approaches will be considered."
>
> Better: "If fewer than 30 communities yield sequenceable DNA after the drought treatment
> (risk moderate: 22 of 25 did in the pilot, Fig. 3), the Davis group extends sampling to the two
> wetter plots, which keeps the drought-history gradient while raising biomass. A null slope would
> itself reject the history hypothesis in favour of the composition model, which is Objective 3's
> starting point."

At most one bounded moonshot, marked as such, in the last block (craft §9.4).

**How it fails.** The technique-as-hammer block, "we will apply method X to system Y", with no
question that X answers. Tasks with no group named (machine-flagged). Runs of "We will …" that
hide who acts (machine-flagged). Methods described at the level of "we will use machine
learning". No stated success criterion, so a referee cannot judge feasibility. Fallbacks gathered
at the end of the plan, far from the claims they protect.

### 4.7 Item 3: the collaboration (¾–1 page)

**What it does.** It answers criterion C in one place, which the regulation's item 3 requires
("the respective roles"). Make it the last subsection of item 3, under a heading with
"collaboration" in it. The tests a referee applies, and how funded proposals pass them, are in
§7. The section holds:

- **Why this pairing**: one sentence per group on what only it brings, then the sentence that
  the question needs both.
- **A roles table**: tasks as rows, groups as columns, with *lead* or *contributes* in each cell.
  It should agree with the lead lines in §4.6.
- **The interface tasks**: the one or two co-led tasks where one group's output is the other's
  input, with the month of each hand-off.
- **The mechanics, in numbers**: visits (who, how long, which year; the BSF funds visits of more
  than two full working days), meeting cadence, shared repositories, co-supervised students, and
  the joint papers you expect.
- **History**, if any. What the pair produced before and what this grant adds. A previous BSF
  grant without joint papers must be addressed here (§7, §10).
- **Governance in two or three sentences.** Who decides changes of direction, and how
  authorship is settled.

**How it fails.** "The two groups have complementary expertise and will meet regularly." A roles
table that disagrees with the tasks. A collaboration paragraph that would read the same for any
other pair of labs.

### 4.8 Item 4: risk analysis and alternative paths (½ page)

**What it does.** It holds only the risks that cross objectives. The fallbacks for each claim
are already in its block (§4.6). Keep the heading even when the plan is low-risk, because
referees are asked about "a fallback plan if so required".

- **Cross-cutting technical risks**, in a short table of risk, likelihood, effect and
  mitigation. Examples are a shared instrument, a permit or field season, recruiting the student,
  a dataset licence, and **the hand-off between the groups**: what happens if one side's input is
  late. The usual mitigation is decoupling: each group can work on shared archived data or
  simulated inputs until the hand-off arrives.
- **Conceptual risk**, in a paragraph. If the central hypothesis is wrong, what does the project
  still establish? A negative result that settles a live question is a result (craft §9.5).
- **Why the risk is worth taking**, in one sentence.

For mathematical and theoretical plans, the risk plan is usually built into the objectives: a
ladder of problems, some within reach, some harder, one very hard, so that partial success is
visible (craft §14). Say which results stand if the main conjecture resists. The regulation asks
for item 4 "only in those fields in which it is relevant", but keep the heading and say this
in two sentences. Do not repeat the per-objective pitfalls. Point to them.

**How it fails.** A generic list ("delays may occur"). Every risk rated low. The same fallbacks
pasted from the blocks.

### 4.9 Item 5: resources (½–¾ page)

**What it does.** It shows that the project as a whole is already resourced. The BSF grant is
mostly "given to support the cooperation" and often "cannot support the entire research project"
(FAQ). It also answers criterion D and criterion C(b), any essential facility one side provides
to the other.

Use two columns or two short paragraphs, **Israel** and **U.S.**, each covering:

- **Personnel**: everyone on the project, with role and effort, and who pays for them, whether
  this grant or another source. They should match the people in the timeline and the budget.
- **Equipment and facilities** relevant to this project, named: instruments, compute, field sites,
  cohorts, collections, datasets, with access arrangements where they are not your own.
- **Other funding** supporting related work, and one sentence on why it does not overlap. It
  must be consistent with the "current grants" list in the CV form (§10).
- **What one side provides to the other**, flagged as such. That is criterion C(b).

**How it fails.** "Both laboratories are fully equipped." Other grants omitted, which reads
either as hiding overlap or as an unfunded plan. People in the timeline who appear nowhere here
or in the budget.

### 4.10 Closing (¼ page)

Do not end on resources or risk. Close with three or four sentences: what will be known at the
end, why these two groups now, and what it opens, including the next joint step. The last
sentence of the plan is the last thing a referee reads before writing the review. Then add the
one-line generative-AI note (§3).

### 4.11 The objection audit

Run this before submitting. In public reviews of modest grants, critiques raised even against
funded proposals came back in the same few forms. Under majority-Excellent, each of them costs
a grade unless the plan answers it in the text:

- **"Others have used this approach"**: answer by naming the closest prior work and saying what
  is new here, which is the reviewer form's "transformative … not repetitive";
- **"The approach misses a class of answers"**: for example, a screen that cannot see essential
  genes; say what is missed and why it does not matter, or how you cover it;
- **"Too ambitious for the time and money"**: §3 of `small_grants.md`; mark stretch goals as
  stretch;
- **"What counts as success?"**: the success criterion per objective;
- **"How will the hard step be done?"**: an approach sentence for every claim, not only a goal;
- **"Who does what?"**: the lead line per task, §7;
- **"The interpretation is not unique"**: the alternative explanation and the comparison that
  rules it out.

A study of how reviewers score proposals found that the risks a reviewer perceived predicted the
score more strongly than the strengths did (Gallo and Schmaling, 2022). That is the general form
of the majority-Excellent rule.

Declined proposals in the same sources failed differently. They had too many problems, too
little detail on each, no strategy for the key step, and undefined success. Funded ones had
weaknesses with a mitigation already in the text.

### 4.13 Starting from an ISF plan

Israeli applicants often start from an Israel Science Foundation plan. One thing to check before
reusing one, and four to change.

**Check the overlap rules first.** The ISF's guidelines (November 2025, as distributed by Tel
Aviv University's research authority) accept only proposals substantially different from any
submitted to or funded by another body, and the applicant undertakes not to submit a similar
proposal elsewhere while one is under review. Read the rule in the current ISF call, and ask your
research authority, before the same project goes to both. The BSF asks for all current grants in
the CV form and resources in item 5 (§10, §4.9), so overlap is visible to referees either way.

**What changes.**

1. **Single-PI independence becomes interdependence.** The ISF scores a PI who can lead the
   research independently. The BSF scores two groups whose parts cannot stand alone. A recycled
   ISF plan with a partner's tasks added reads as two independent projects, and fails the removal
   test (§7). Rebuild the objectives so each needs both groups (§4.4).
2. **The roles go into item 3.** Every task gets a lead line, and the collaboration subsection
   (§4.7) is new.
3. **Items 4 and 5 are new headings.** The ISF's pitfalls inside the methods can stay where they
   are, as the block pitfalls of §4.6. Item 4 then adds the cross-cutting risks, and item 5 the
   resources of **both** sides.
4. **The first pages change.** The opening must say why two groups (§4.1), and the background
   must cover both groups' literatures at the same depth (§4.2), where an ISF background covers
   one.

## 5. Who reads what

The evaluation runs in five steps, in the BSF's words: "Applications Distributed to Panels →
Panels Select Reviewers → Ad-Hoc Reviews Collected → Panels Rank the Applications → Board
Determines Grants No. & Average Size."

| Reader | How many | Expertise | Sees | Does |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ad-hoc referees | **3–5 per application**, averaging **4.5** in the 2024/25 cycle | experts in the subfield. Of 1,409 reviews in the last cycle, **17 % were Israeli, 38 % American, 44 % European or other** | the full compiled PDF: plan, abstract, impact statement, CVs, publications, budget, letters | written review against criteria A–G plus a grade (§6) |
| **The adviser** for your proposal | one | a senior scientist in the field, Israeli or American | the application and all reviews | proposes referees, then **summarises the reviews and adds their own assessment** for the panel. Some proposals get a second opinion from the field head |
| Scientific panel (advisers and field heads) | one per field | U.S.–Israeli scientists, senior, covering several areas | the application, the reviews and the adviser's summary | discusses with BSF staff present; declares the Excellent proposals fundable and ranks them |
| Board of Governors | — | — | the ranking | sets the number and average size of grants, normally capping the rate at about 25 %; does not intervene in selection |

**How referees are chosen.** Each field has a field head and advisers. The field head assigns
your proposal to an adviser. The adviser proposes referees, and the panel meets to discuss the
names and add more, **"including 2 from the list the applicants recommended"** (BSF, 2025). Two
of your roughly 4.5 referees are therefore likely to come from your list. The BSF also uses the
bibliography to find referees (§10).

What follows from this table for the writing:

- **Write for one adviser.** One panel member carries your proposal into the meeting with a
  summary of the reviews and their own view. Make the abstract, the first page and the
  collaboration section the summary you want that person to give.
- **Write for a mostly non-Israeli audience.** Four in five referees are outside Israel. Do not
  assume familiarity with Israeli systems, sites, institutions or datasets. Introduce them in a
  clause, as you would a method.
- **There is no generalist-only stage.** Every scored page is read by an expert, so craft §1's
  stage-4 check becomes "can an expert fault any step?". The panel, however, ranks across
  subfields from reviews, so **the abstract and the first page must give a neighbouring-field
  panel member the question, the approach and the collaboration in plain words**
  (`small_grants.md` §1).
- **Referees are partly yours to choose.** You must suggest six (§10). The panel selects the
  referees, but a good list makes it likely that at least some of them know both the method and
  the system.
- **Reviews come back to you.** "About 2 weeks" after the results, all the reviews and panel
  summaries become available online, edited to remove reviewer identity and overall ratings.
  There is **no appeal mechanism**, but BSF staff will discuss the outcome. That is the input to
  a resubmission (§11).
- **No interview and no rebuttal.** An ambiguity is resolved by the referee, once.

## 6. The evaluation criteria and the grade scale

**The criteria, as the BSF's evaluation page states them:**

1. "Scientific and technological merit of the research program, including its originality,
   importance, novelty and uniqueness, and whether the research is on the cutting edge of its
   field;"
2. "The strength of the collaborative arrangements between the Israeli and US investigators and
   the manner in which the joint research effort manifests itself, e.g., joint contribution,
   complementary research, exchange of materials or equipment, use of special research
   facilities, exchange of scientists, etc.;"
3. "Anticipated benefits in advancing and developing the scientific area in question, and/or
   possible social and economic benefits;"
4. "The suitability of the investigators and the means at their disposal, such as equipment and
   facilities;"
5. "Scientific and cooperation record in existing or previous BSF grants (when relevant)."

"The selection process … is based solely on the scientific quality of the applications, and on
the expected level of collaboration between the PIs."

**What the referee is actually asked.** The reviewer guidelines are the document the reader
holds, so they outrank the summary above on what gets written. Each referee is asked to analyse:

- **A) Intellectual Merit**: "special attention to the originality and novelty", and "the
  working hypotheses, the work plan and methodology, including a fallback plan if so required".
- **B) Importance & Broader Impact**: impact on the field if successful; "Is the proposed
  research transformative (it builds on previous research findings but it is not repetitive)?";
  secondary outcomes, such as medical or societal benefit.
- **C) Cooperation**: "a. Does the proposed research in the different laboratories consist of
  interdependent, complimentary [sic] parts of a single program? Will the outcomes of the collaboration
  be more than the sum of the individual efforts? b. Does one of the PIs provide essential
  research facilities, field materials or advanced equipment to the collaborating PI?"
- **D) PI Suitability and scientific environments**: qualifications and facilities.
- **E) Time Schedule**: "Is it realistic or is it too ambitious?"
- **F) Strengths and Weaknesses**, in short.
- **G) Overall Recommendation**: "your overall impression of the expected cooperation" plus a
  final grade.

**The criteria map** (`review_protocol.md` §1), with the shorthands used throughout this guide
and in findings:

| Shorthand | Criterion | Scored by | From |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Intellectual merit: originality, novelty, hypotheses, plan, methodology, fallback | referees → panel | plan items 1–4 |
| **B** | Importance and broader impact, "transformative … not repetitive" | referees → panel | plan items 1–2, Broader Impacts Statement, abstract |
| **C** | Cooperation: interdependent single programme, more than the sum, essential facilities exchanged | referees → panel | plan item 3 and §7 section, letters of collaboration, previous joint output |
| **D** | PI suitability and environment | referees → panel | CVs, publications, plan item 5 |
| **E** | Time schedule realistic | referees | work schedule form, plan item 3 |
| **R** | Previous BSF record, joint products | panel | CV form, joint-publications file |
| **X** | Required content and format (unscored, enforced) | BSF office, panel | everything in §3–§4 |

**The grade scale, and the funding threshold.** Referees grade E, VG, G, F or P. In the BSF's own
words: "typically, only applications that score high on the cooperation and receive 'Excellent'
merit scores by a majority of the reviewers are recommended for funding." "Excellent" is
"reserved for near-perfect proposals where there are few or no critical remarks". "Very Good"
proposals "score high on intellectual merit, work plan, PI strength etc. but reviewers do have
some remarks that require consideration … these proposals will likely not be elected for
funding". "Good" carries concerns about hypotheses, plan or method, and "additional challenges
with respect to organization and presentation, often also with use of the English language".
"Fair" proposals "typically lack a clear research question and hypotheses" and "should not be
considered for resubmission".

**What the panel does with the grades.** BSF staff describe it this way (2025): in the regular
programme, proposals graded **Excellent** are declared fundable after the panel's discussion. In
the start-up programme, **Excellent or Very Good** proposals are. All fundable proposals are
ranked, and the Board's budget decides how far down the ranking the money reaches. Some fundable
proposals are always left unfunded.

**Give the enthusiastic referee something to quote.** The guidelines tell a referee giving an
Excellent that "it is essential that you express your enthusiasm by providing strong arguments
that highlight the proposal's strengths". A referee who likes the proposal must still write the
argument down, and the adviser repeats it to the panel. Give them two or three sentences that
survive being quoted: why now, the enabling result only this team has, and what the two groups
can do together that neither can alone.

Four consequences that drive the rest of this guide:

1. **The grant is lost by a single plausible objection.** With about four referees, a majority
   of Excellents means three. One reviewer's "some remarks that require consideration" is a
   Very Good, and a second one ends it. Write to leave nothing that needs consideration: a
   fallback on every risky step, the obvious alternative explanation answered where it arises,
   and no claim an expert would doubt left without support (`small_grants.md` §2).
2. **Cooperation is a separate gate.** "Score high on the cooperation" *and* Excellent merit. A
   merit-Excellent proposal with a thin collaboration is not funded (§7).
3. **Clarity and English are on the scale.** Organisation, presentation and English appear in the
   definition of "Good". A professional language edit is a scoring action.
4. **Hypotheses are expected.** Absent hypotheses are named in the definition of "Fair". State
   them as testable claims, even in a computational or theoretical plan, where "hypothesis" can
   be a conjecture or a claim about what a method will reveal.

## 7. Writing the collaboration

The cooperation criterion is where BSF proposals most often differ from the same science
submitted alone. The general advice is in `small_grants.md` §4. Here is how to make it
visible within the BSF's own documents.

**Make interdependence the structure, not a paragraph.** Item 3 should read as one programme
with handoffs:

- Every task, or every work package if tasks are short, names **which group does it** and
  **what it receives from the other**. "The Rehovot group measures X (Task 2.1); these
  measurements constrain the model the Austin group fits in Task 2.2, whose predictions choose
  the conditions for Task 2.3 back in Rehovot." Write "we" only where both groups act together.
- Include **one figure** with the two groups as columns or lanes and arrows for data, samples,
  models and people crossing between them. It answers criterion C(a) at a glance, as the
  project-design figure of craft §9.1 does for the objectives.
- Give each partner **one sentence on what they bring that the other lacks**: a system, an
  instrument, a dataset, a method or a population. Then add the sentence saying the question
  needs both. That pair answers "more than the sum of the individual efforts" directly.
- If one side provides "essential research facilities, field materials or advanced equipment",
  criterion C(b), name the item, its location and who will use it when.

**State the mechanics** in a short collaboration section: exchange visits with who, how long and
when (the BSF funds visits of **more than two full working days**); co-supervised students;
shared data and code; regular joint meetings; who integrates results and writes joint papers.
The BSF "expects" joint publications. Say which papers you expect to co-author.

**Show that both of you wrote it.** "The BSF also expects that the PIs from both countries will
be involved in preparing the application, particularly the research plan. When it is apparent
that this was not the case, it is considered to indicate a lack of collaboration and it hinders
the chances." Evidence: a single register throughout, both groups' preliminary results in item
1 or 3, both groups' methods described with the same depth, and letters of collaboration that
each describe the writer's own role in the writer's own words (§10).

**Uneven budgets are fine, uneven roles are not.** The way the money is divided "has little
bearing on the chances of success", but each PI's letter must explain a zero request (§9, §10).
A partner with no budget and a vague role reads as a name on the cover page.

**Previous BSF grant together?** Then the joint-publications file is read as evidence for
criteria C and R. A previous joint grant with no joint papers "reduces" the chances (FAQ). Address
it head-on in the collaboration section: what was produced, what is in press, why it was slower.

**A long partnership has the opposite problem.** A practitioner talk hosted by a university
grants office reports a standing tension. A partnership with many years of joint papers is
evidence that the collaboration works, but it invites the question of what is new. Say what this
grant lets the pair do that their past work could not.

**Tests a reviewer applies, and how funded multi-site proposals pass them.** These come from the
BSF's own wording, analogous schemes that score collaboration (NSF CISE collaboration plans,
HFSP, DFG–Israel DIP, NIH multi-PI leadership plans) and funded multi-site NSF proposals that
were made public.

| Test | What fails | What funded proposals do |
|---|---|---|
| **Removal test:** delete one group's tasks. Does any objective survive intact? | it does, so it is two parallel projects under one title. HFSP calls this "concerted action" and will not fund it | every objective needs both groups |
| **Who leads each task?** | tasks with no group named, or every task in one lab | every task or sub-task ends with a lead line: "led by the Haifa group, with the Michigan student" |
| **Where do the groups meet?** | only in a closing paragraph | at least one **interface task**, co-led, where one group's output is the other's input, with a month attached |
| **Why these two?** | goodwill, or "complementary expertise" left unexplained | a **scientific reason for the pairing**: two field sites, two populations, an instrument on one side and a theory on the other |
| **How will it run?** | "we will meet regularly" | numbers: visits by whom, how long, in which year; meeting cadence; shared repository; co-supervised student |
| **Are both real partners?** | one side is a figurehead or a service provider | both sides carry scientific tasks and appear in preliminary results; a facility-providing partner is named as such, criterion C(b) |
| **Do the money and the roles agree?** | the budget, or the letters, tell a different story from the plan | budget lines and letters match the tasks; travel lines match the visits |

**Money to the U.S. partner is read as a signal.** The BSF says the division of funds is not a
criterion, and that it "may affect only the size of the grant". Its own 2025 tips slide,
however, lists money for the American partner under collaboration, next to a joint work plan and
detailed letters. Where the U.S. partner requests nothing, the letter must say how they will
take part.

**Keep governance short.** NIH-style leadership plans with steering committees and arbitration
look out of proportion on a two-lab grant of this size. Two or three sentences cover it: who
decides on changes of direction, how authorship is settled, and how the groups resolve
disagreement.

## 8. Abstract, broader impacts, and lay abstract

Three short documents with three different readers.

**Abstract (250 words, sent to referees, public if funded).** The file opens with the heading
"Abstract", the full title ("brief, meaningful and suitable for use in the general media"), the
application number from the system, and the PIs' names and affiliations. The text must be
"informative to scientists in the same or related fields" and must include "a statement of the
project's potential contribution to the research done in that field". Because the panel ranks
across subfields, this is the text every panel member reads. Spend it on: the question in one
sentence; the instability or barrier (craft §2); the approach and *why two groups*; the
objectives in a phrase each; and the contribution. Do not end on methods.

**Broader Impacts Statement (about 250 words, sent to referees).** Same header block, with the
heading "Broader Impacts Statement". It should "address the broader impact and importance
(social and/or economic and/or scientific value)". This feeds criterion B, so lead with the
scientific consequence for the field, with a *because* (craft §8). Social or economic value
comes next where it is real and specific. Training of students and postdocs in two systems
belongs here too. **This is not NSF's Broader Impacts criterion**, and the BSF statement is not
scored as a separate merit axis. Do not paste an NSF section in unchanged (§13).

**Lay abstract (not sent to reviewers).** Used only if funded, for the website and PR. Title in
lay terms, the PIs, and why it matters "to humanity, the environment, or … the scientific
field". Write it last and keep it accurate.

## 9. Budget

- **$300,000 maximum including 15 % overhead on all items**, in U.S. dollars at current prices,
  as one budget per institution requesting funds. Start-up: a fixed $90,000 for two years, and
  $90,000 to each partner when both are junior.
- **What you are likely to get depends on the shape of the request**, not only its total. The
  maximum is "expected" for **experimental work over four years with funds divided equally**
  between the U.S. and Israeli partners. Theoretical work with one PI requesting funds is
  "expected to be only $150,000", somewhat more if both request funds, "but never twice as
  much". A shorter project does not raise the annual amount. Equal division is "awarded more
  funding", yet the division "has little bearing on the chances of success". Request what the
  plan needs within those expectations, and do not shorten the project to concentrate money.
- **What grants actually come to.** The BSF's 2025 guidance: the basic budget is up to $75,000 a
  year for both PIs together. Theoretical research needing only personnel receives about
  $37,000–50,000 a year. Research needing lab or field work receives about $50,000–75,000 a
  year. The average in the last cycle was $296,000 over four years. Awarded amounts in the BSF's
  public grant database, 2020–2025 (§16), agree: medians of about $178,000 in mathematics,
  $189,000 in computer science and $170,000 in economics, against $284,000 in the life
  sciences. **Plan the science for the grant you will receive, not for the cap.** A
  theory-only plan that needs $300,000 to work is a plan the referees will call too ambitious.
- **Budget years start in October or later.** Do not add price escalation; the BSF applies its
  own.
- **No PI salary.** Israeli and U.S. PIs "CANNOT receive salary or supplement their salary", and
  that includes summer salary and adjunct faculty who live on external funding. Postdocs,
  students (salary, fringe and tuition), other scientists, and group technical staff who depend
  on external funding are eligible, in proportion to their effort. Experts and consultants are
  paid by invoice, and consultants with permanent full-time positions not at all.
- **Equipment** is permanent, project-critical equipment only, never general-purpose, and is
  capped at **20 % of the total budget**. The BSF owns it and typically releases it to the
  institution at the end. Consult the BSF if more than two PIs request equipment.
- **Travel** is for exchange visits between the groups, in both directions, by any team member.
  Visits must be **more than two full days** excluding weekends and holidays. Fares are economy
  and fixed-date, with per diem only for days spent together. Conferences in the U.S. or Israel
  are now eligible. Third-country travel needs prior approval. A partner requesting only travel
  adds it to the initiator's budget and submits no budget form. Travel money cannot be moved to
  other lines without approval.
- **Publication charges** are up to $1,000 a year and $3,000 per project, **only for joint
  publications** of the applicants.
- **Justify every major item, including travel.** Tie each person and each trip to a task in
  item 3 (craft §12).

## 10. CV, publications, letters, references and suggested reviewers

**CV (online form, per PI).** Academic background including the **names of PhD supervisors
and postdoc hosts**, and professional experience. List **all current grants and contracts**,
including BSF grants, and keep them consistent with the resources in item 5. Answer the
previous-BSF-grant question, and whether it produced joint refereed publications.

**Publications (upload, per PI, 5 pages, under the same 12 pt / 1.5 / 2 cm rules).** The heading
carries the investigator's name and "Publications". List mainly relevant and/or recent work
(last five years), numbered, **ordered by year**, with **your own name in bold**. Give all
authors up to five, and the first three plus "et al." beyond that, naming the collaboration
where it matters. Full title, journal, year and pages. Give the status of anything unpublished.
Peer-reviewed work first, plus the output types normal in your field, such as conference
proceedings in computer science. The general rules in craft §11 apply, with one BSF-specific
difference: the list is ordered by year rather than grouped by theme.

**Joint publications from a previous BSF grant (per PI, if a grant was active or ended in the
last five years).** Only items co-authored by the PIs from *both* countries, or by their groups.
Exclude papers that merely acknowledge the BSF. Patents and workshops may be listed in free
form. Collaborators from the same previous grant upload one list.

**Letter of collaboration (per PI, signed, on letterhead).** It must say whether the PI read or
helped prepare the application and give "a detailed description of the PI's future role and
contribution", and, if no funds are requested, how they will contribute without them. These
letters are evidence for criterion C. Write them as such: specific tasks, specific facilities,
specific visits, in each PI's own voice.

**Letter from consultant (optional).** On letterhead, signed, describing the consultant's role.
Consultants are added automatically to the reviewers-to-avoid list.

**References.** The heading is "References". Use full details, because the BSF uses this list to
**find reviewers**: all authors up to four, otherwise the first three plus "et al.", full title,
journal, year and pages, numbered or bulleted. Any standard citation style is fine. The list may
sit at the end of the plan, outside the 15 pages, if you tick the checkbox.

**Suggested reviewers (online form).** **At least six**, more welcome, **no more than three from
Israel**, each with affiliation, email and specialty. Not permitted: co-authors of any paper with
up to five authors, or of any grant, with any PI in the last five years; thesis, PhD or postdoc
advisers or hosts, and former students; departmental colleagues, including colleagues at an
institution where a PI worked in the last five years or recently visited; anyone connected with
the application, including consultants. You may also name **up to three people to avoid**.
Choose names that cover both halves of the project, the system and the method. A list made
entirely of one side's community invites reviews that see only half of the collaboration.
**This list matters more than it looks.** The BSF says the panel adds "2 from the list the
applicants recommended" to the referees it chooses (§5), so about two of four or five reviews
come from it. Put first the names you most want, who know the field and will engage with
the collaboration. Choose people who will write a substantive review on time, not the most
famous names.

## 11. Resubmission, continuation, and the outcome codes

**How many times.** A revised application may be submitted a **second time**, unless the
decision letter says it is not recommended. An application unfunded in two competitions **may
not be submitted a third time**, and doing so bars the applicant from the next **two** years.
The one exception is a revised application whose letter carries code **N3E**: "It is an
excellent proposal and eligible for support, but its relative rank within the list of eligible
applications was not sufficiently high". That code appears in the upper right of the results
letter, and only that application may go a third time. "An application that is not allowed to
be resubmitted, and is submitted with minor changes as a new application, is likely to be
rejected!"

**Changing area.** An application may not be resubmitted the next year under a different area
of research unless the panel recommended it or the BSF office gave permission. Interdisciplinary
applications need pre-approval too. An unapproved move is rejected and blocks the application.
Note the alternating years: a proposal from an even-year area next competes two years later
unless it moves. A psychobiology proposal that is not funded cannot come back the next year as
psychology.

**Consecutive years with a different topic** need no approval.

**The resubmission letter** goes to the BSF staff and to reviewers of the previous application.
The new round goes "mostly to new reviewers, but also to some of the reviewers of the first
submission". It has a heading "Resubmission Letter" and a maximum of five pages. Explain what
changed and how it answers the reviewers and the panel summary. "You may copy only the relevant
sentences from the reviews, but avoid copying entire reviews." Craft §13 has the general rules.
At the BSF the panel summary matters as much as the individual reviews, because the panel did
the ranking. Answer its stated reason first. Then re-check timeliness and literature, as the
BSF itself advises.

**Decide first whether to resubmit or reframe.** The rejection letter carries every external
review and the panel's summary and recommendations. Read them, wait a week, and read them again.
Then sort the weaknesses into two piles. **Fixable** means feasibility detail, clarity, missing
fallbacks, thin collaboration mechanics or English. **Fatal** means the question's importance,
the fit to the panel, or the collaboration itself was doubted. With mostly fixable weaknesses
and praise for significance, resubmit. With fatal ones, reframe as a genuinely new proposal, and
remember that a lightly edited old one is rejected (above). Because attempts are capped, this is
the most consequential decision in the cycle.

**How a resubmission letter is built.** This pattern is drawn from NIH resubmission
introductions, grants-office guidance and the journal-response literature. It is adapted to the
BSF's five pages and mixed audience of old and new reviewers:

1. **Identify the previous application** by number and title, and say in one sentence what the
   panel valued, if it said so.
2. **Summarise the major changes** in a short list before any point-by-point answer. New
   reviewers read only this part closely.
3. **Answer the panel summary first**, then the individual reviews. Group shared concerns and
   attribute them ("R1, R3"). With five pages there is room for minor points too, so answer
   them.
4. **Per item:** the critique in a short quote or a fair paraphrase, then a direct answer (agree,
   partly agree, or disagree), then the change and **where it is** (section and page).
5. **Disagree with evidence, never with the reviewer.** Address the concern under the critique,
   and offer an alternative. "The reviewer missed", "as clearly stated" and "we disagree" without
   a reason are the phrases to cut.
6. **New results** go where they answer a critique.
7. **Every change claimed is actually in the plan.** Old reviewers check.

The BSF's instructions say nothing about marking changes in the resubmitted plan. NIH has
forbidden it since 2024, and that rule does not apply here. Ask the BSF office before adding
change bars, and keep the plan readable for the new reviewers either way.

**Continuation of a previous BSF grant.** Upload a one-page **Progress Report**: previous grant
number, applicants' names, title. Mark the continuation on the cover page. Contact the BSF if it
is a resubmission of a continuation.

## 12. Start-up grants

A two-year track for junior PIs: **no more than 10 years since the PhD or MD**, whichever came
earlier, at submission. Extensions are possible for documented parental leave, illness or
other interruptions, but **must be requested before you start preparing the application**.

- The typical shape is a junior PI plus a senior PI, **and the senior PI is not funded**, apart
  from travel paid through the junior PI's institution. Alternatively two junior PIs, one per
  country, with or without an unfunded senior.
- Budget is a fixed $90,000, or $90,000 each when both PIs are junior.
- **Preliminary results are not required.** The reviewer guidelines say start-ups are "otherwise
  reviewed in the same fashion and using the same criteria as regular proposals", and that the
  panel gives them "special consideration". Do not read this as a lower bar on the plan. Read it
  as permission to argue feasibility from the PI's training, the senior partner's environment
  and a smaller, sharper aim.
- One start-up per career. A failed start-up may be resubmitted within the eligibility window,
  and afterwards the PI applies as a regular applicant.
- The senior partner's role should be concrete: facilities, mentoring and specific tasks. A
  senior name with no role reads as decoration and costs criterion C.
- **The bar is lower in one documented way.** Excellent *or* Very Good start-ups are fundable
  after panel discussion, against Excellent only in the regular track (§6). Success rates were
  38 % in 2024 (12 grants) and 40 % in 2021. An applicant on a start-up may not also submit a
  regular proposal in the same cycle.

**What to write in place of preliminary data.** This draws on NIH R21 and early-stage
investigator guidance, NSF CRII, and practice in mentored awards:

- **A feasibility argument anyway.** Use literature, pilot design, tools that already exist, or
  the senior partner's data. Practitioner guides for early-career grants agree that one
  feasibility figure, even a modest one, raises confidence. Reviewers also question missing data
  that would have been cheap to produce.
- **An exit statement.** Say what the two-year result enables, normally the question of a
  regular BSF proposal. NSF CRII scores exactly this ("necessary and critical steps" to launch
  the career), and it is the BSF's stated purpose for the track.
- **The senior partner in the work plan, not only in the team paragraph**, with named tasks, a
  meeting cadence and deliverables.
- **The ideas visibly the junior's.** Mentoring-award reviewers penalise plans in which the
  senior drives the science, and Emmy Noether-style schemes score independence explicitly. Write
  the aims in the junior PI's voice, and put the senior's contribution in facilities, access,
  methods and advice.
- **Two-way contribution.** One sentence on what the junior brings to the senior's group or to
  the binational pair. This also answers criterion C.
- **Scope for $90,000 over two years**: one or two objectives, a milestone every six months, and
  no four-year plan compressed into two.

## 13. NSF-BSF and NEH-BSF: a different game

The BSF also runs joint programmes with NSF directorates and with NEH. **These are NSF, or NEH,
proposals.** The U.S. PI submits to NSF under NSF rules, with "NSF-BSF:" prefixed to the title.
The Israeli PI submits **the identical PDF** to the BSF a few days after the NSF deadline. NSF
reviews it in the regular core-programme competition, under NSF's criteria. "BSF watches over
compliance by Israeli applicants, but does not evaluate the scientific merit." The BSF only
screens whether the Israeli role is substantial enough to fund, and it funds every Israeli whose
U.S. partner is funded.

What changes for the writing:

- **Write to NSF's two criteria, Intellectual Merit and Broader Impacts.** Broader Impacts is
  scored by NSF and a missing one gets the proposal returned without review. The BSF's own call
  documents warn Israeli applicants that the term has "a greatly different meaning" there than
  in BSF or ISF applications.
- **Make the Israeli role critical and visible.** Explain "why the contribution of the Israeli PI
  is critical", and describe the roles in the work plan and timeline. The Israeli PI cannot
  appear on the NSF cover page and is listed as a non-NSF-funded collaborator, so the NSF
  submission **must include the Israeli PI's biography (NSF format), budget and budget
  justification (BSF format), and a collaboration plan** as supplementary documents.
- **Calibrate ambition to the NSF programme, not to the BSF.** A BSF tips presentation advises
  that in regular NSF programmes "incremental progress programs have better chances with the
  panel", and that a genuinely transformative idea should be checked with the programme director.
  Programme directors, not the panel, make the final decisions, and may weigh portfolio balance
  and the U.S. PI's existing funding.
- **Limits on the Israeli side.** Up to two NSF-BSF applications per academic year (1 October –
  30 September), and no more than two proposals or grants at any one time. The Israeli award is
  capped at 400,000 NIS for experimental work and 330,000 NIS for theoretical or computational
  work, which the BSF system enters as $114,000 and $94,000 a year at its fixed rate. The cap
  rises by up to 50 % when more than one Israeli group is involved. Duration follows NSF's
  award.
- **Topic eligibility questions go to the NSF programme director**, through the U.S. PI. "BSF will
  not respond to any query regarding topic eligibility."

Use `grant-review` with the NSF solicitation as the criteria source for these. This guide's §4–§12
do not apply.

**The regulations for your own year are the authority, not this file.** The BSF updates them each
cycle. Things that are easy to get wrong from older advice or from colleagues:

- **The cap is $300,000 from the 2026 cycle**, raised from $250,000 in the 2024 and 2025
  regulations. Overhead is 15 %, inside the cap. The cap is not the typical award (§9).
- **The area of research alternates by year**, so the field you applied in two years ago may not
  be open this year.
- **Conferences in the U.S. or Israel are now fundable travel**, and the regulations say "now".
- **Institutions are no longer required to supplement start-up grants.**
- **The generative-AI disclosure is new** and encouraged.
- **The success-rate figure moves.** The statistics page says the Board "limited the success
  rate to 25 %" from 2018 to raise grant sizes. The 2024/25 exact-sciences cycle reached 35 %,

- **The fundability bar differs by track:** Excellent in the regular track, Excellent or Very
  Good in start-up.
- **Third-submission bar.** A third submission without an N3E code bars the PIs for two years.

## 15. Review overlay

How `grant-review`'s ladder (`review_protocol.md`) changes for a BSF draft. Run the general
rubric and apply these deltas. Criteria shorthands are from §6.

**Stage 0, census.** `grant_check.py --profile bsf --census`. Expect `research_plan`, `abstract`
and `broader_impacts`, plus the optional parts. A plan "at" 15 pages means every suggestion names
what it displaces.

**Stage 1, fit.** Add: the area is open this year and the panel is the intended one (§2); every PI
is eligible and not over the one-at-a-time limits, with consultants correctly classified; the
submission count is allowed (§11); and for a start-up, the 10-year window or an approved
extension (§12). **Shape:** two groups doing one programme in both countries. A one-lab project
with an advisor attached fails here, not at stage 5. Cost: C, and the whole application.
**Size:** the aim is sized to roughly $300k across two groups plus stated existing resources
(`small_grants.md` §3).

**Stage 2, problem.** As general. Add: **hypotheses are stated** as testable claims, because
their absence is in the definition of "Fair" (§6). Add the **page-1 test** (§4.1): the first page
carries the question, the central hypothesis or testable objective, the objectives in a line
each, and one sentence on why two groups. Cost: A, and the adviser's summary.

**Stage 4, reader sufficiency.** Replace the generalist-stage test with two tests. (a) **Expert
test:** read item 3 as a referee in each half of the project. Every step that referee would
question has its justification or fallback within a paragraph. Cost: A, per referee. (b)
**Panel test:** the abstract plus the first page alone give the question, the approach, the
two-group logic and the contribution. Cost: A and C at ranking.

**Stage 5, criteria coverage.** Find an address for each of A, B, C, D, E, R and X. The BSF-specific
items:

- **C, cooperation.** *Test:* the seven tests of §7. The removal test, a lead group per task,
  at least one co-led interface task, a scientific reason for the pairing, mechanics stated in
  numbers, two real partners, and money and letters that agree with the plan. *Cost:* C, a
  separate gate on funding. *Suggest:* where roles are missing, ask the authors who does each
  task. Do not assign them. The checker flags tasks that name no group (`task-owner`).
- **The objection audit** (§4.11). *Test:* write down the three most plausible objections an expert
  in each half would raise, and find each one's answer in the text. *Cost:* A or E, one grade
  each under majority-Excellent. *Suggest:* the if-then pitfall item in the objective's block.
- **Quotable strengths** (§6). *Test:* two or three sentences an enthusiastic referee could lift
  into an Excellent review. *Cost:* A and B at ranking.
- **Budget realism** (§9). *Test:* the plan's scope matches the grant this kind of project
  actually receives, not the cap. *Cost:* E.
- **Feasibility is scored, so demonstrate it.** This is the opposite of the ERC Step-1 rule.
  *Test:* each objective has preliminary data, a pilot, prior work by one of the groups, or an
  explicit argument for why the method will work; a fallback exists for each risky step. *Cost:*
  A and E. Start-ups are exempt from preliminary data, not from the argument (§12).
- **Item 5 carries the unfunded part of the project.** *Test:* resources name equipment, other
  grants and people on both sides, consistent with the CV forms. *Cost:* D and E.
- **The Broader Impacts Statement answers B in the BSF's sense** (§8), and is not a pasted NSF
  section.
- **Previous joint grant.** If the PIs held a BSF grant together, the plan or letters address its
  joint output. *Cost:* R and C.
- **X, the five required items**, each with a visible heading (§4), and the required headings in
  each file (§3). These are machine-checked.

**Stage 6, work plan.** Add: every objective block has the five parts of §4.6, including a
success criterion and pitfalls (machine-flagged as `objective-success` and `objective-pitfalls`);
milestones are results, not activities (§4.5); the **work schedule form agrees with item 3**,
criterion E, "realistic or … too ambitious?"; every person budgeted appears in a task, and vice
versa; exchange visits appear in the timeline; at most one bounded moonshot (craft §9.4).

**Stage 7, mechanics.** Add: **name the acting group** wherever "we" hides which site acts. This is
the craft §10 active-voice rule, and at the BSF it is also criterion C. A run of "We will …"
sentences in item 3 is machine-flagged.

**Stage 8, prose.** Add: an English-quality pass is a scoring action here, because "Good" names
it (§6). Flag non-idiomatic phrasing at stage 8 even when the meaning is clear.

**Stage 9, compliance.** `grant_check.py --profile bsf`. It checks the format rules from source,
including the `\onehalfspacing` line-count trap, word limits, required headings, the five
required items, the PI-salary and overhead patterns, and the generative-AI note. Then the
by-eye list it prints: the reviewer-list conflicts, the letters, budget arithmetic in the online
form, the final generated PDF against Appendix III, and the grants office's internal deadline.

## 16. What funded BSF material looks like

No BSF research plan is public. A search of PI sites, the 552 proposals in the Open Grants
collection, Zenodo, OSF and GitHub found none, and no shared BSF reviews or panel summaries.
What can be measured are the BSF's public grant records, the lay abstracts the applicants
wrote, and the NSF's abstracts of NSF-BSF awards. Use these as a comparison, never as a target.

**Award records** (the BSF grant database, 513 regular grants awarded 2020–2025):

- **Awarded amounts.**
  - By year: median $177,000–308,000. Odd (life-science) years ran higher than even years.
  - By field, as medians with the interquartile range:
    - mathematics $178,000 ($142,000–250,000);
    - computer science $189,000 ($165,000–223,000);
    - economics $170,000;
    - physics and chemistry $236,000;
    - life and medical sciences $284,000 ($228,000–300,000).
  - Some records exceed the stated cap, up to $330,000, presumably after the BSF's own
    adjustments for later years.
- **PIs per grant.** Two in 70 %, three in 22 %, four or more in 8 %. Larger teams are allowed
  but uncommon.
- **Titles.** Median 11 words (interquartile range 8–14). A colon in 19 %, a question in 3 %,
  "mechanism" in 9 %, "novel" or "new" in 5 %. Funded BSF titles are plain declaratives naming
  the system and the process.

**Lay abstracts written by the funded applicants** (coded from the BSF database: 187 regular and 25
start-up, awarded 2023–2025):

| Feature (regex-coded; read as approximate) | Regular | Start-up |
|---|---|---|
| Length, median words (interquartile range) | 276 (217–325) | 274 (219–300) |
| First person "we" | 81 % | 88 % |
| A word of collaboration ("together", "joint", "combine", "complementary", …) | 45 % | 32 % |
| Names both countries in the body | 49 % | 40 % |
| A sentence saying what one side or group does | 18 % | 8 % |
| An explicit reason for this pairing ("complementary", "each group", …) | 7 % | 4 % |
| A question in the first three sentences | 6 % | 0 % |
| "Hypothesis" | 4 % | 0 % |
| "Novel", "for the first time", "breakthrough", "paradigm" | 21 % | 16 % |
| A payoff sentence ("if successful", "will enable", "will inform") | 25 % | 36 % |

The lay abstract is written for the public and is not sent to referees, so it shows how funded
teams frame their project, not what referees read. The pattern matches the NSF-BSF abstracts
below. Most open on the problem in plain terms, few state a hypothesis there, and **fewer than
one in five says what each side does or why the pair is needed**. Referees see that information
in the plan and the letters. The lay abstract is where the BSF's public description of your grant
comes from, so it is worth one sentence there too.

**NSF-BSF award abstracts** (83 unique awards, 2020–2026; NSF rewrites these in its own house
style, so they show NSF convention as much as the applicants'):

| Feature | Share |
|---|---|
| Opens with broad context rather than the question (hand check, n = 24) | ~75 % |
| States the gap or puzzle in the first two sentences (hand check) | 25 % |
| Uses "hypothesis" | 23 % |
| Numbered aims or questions | 28 % |
| "Novel", "for the first time", "unprecedented" or "paradigm" | 29 % |
| "Transformative" | 0 % |
| Names an Israeli institution | 33 % |
| Says concretely what the Israeli side does (hand check) | 13 % |
| Gives a scientific reason for pairing these countries or labs (hand check) | 17 % |

The rare abstracts that name a scientific reason for the pairing stand out. Examples are two
fault systems (one in each country), water sources in both countries, families studied in both
countries, and a division of labour with experiments on one side and modelling on the other.
Their rarity is a reason to write such a sentence, not evidence that panels do not want one.

**What the measurements support, and what they do not.** They support plain titles, a modest
register (no "transformative"), a stated hypothesis where the field uses one, and making the
pairing's reason explicit, which most funded abstracts leave implicit. They do not support any
section-length rule for the research plan. The shares in §4 come from funded NIH and NSF
analogues, not from BSF plans.
